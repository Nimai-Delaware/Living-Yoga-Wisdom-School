What Is Bhakti?

Introduction to the yoga of love and devotion

Essential Questions

  • What is bhakti, and how is it different from belief, emotion, or general spirituality?
  • How does bhakti integrate action, knowledge, and meditation within a single path?
  • Who am I according to bhakti philosophy, and how is the self distinct from body and mind?
  • What does it mean to reorganize life around service rather than self-centered aims?
  • How do practices like chanting, studying sacred texts, and serving others gradually help us remember our relationship with the Divine?

Module 1 — What Bhakti Is

Bhakti is one of the classical paths within the broader yoga tradition. The word yoga means “to link” or “to connect.” At its heart, yoga is about reconnecting the individual self with the Divine.

Within the yoga tradition, different paths emphasize different practices. Bhakti is commonly translated as devotion, but the system of bhakti yoga involves much more than the sentiment of devotion. It is a way of orienting our lives toward the Divine through service, remembrance, meditation, and intentional spiritual practice.

Bhakti teaches that each of us has an eternal, loving relationship with our divine, conscious Source, and that our deepest fulfillment comes from uncovering that relationship. The aim of bhakti is not limited to moral refinement or psychological calm, though those may arise along the way. Bhakti offers a way of understanding who we are, why we struggle, and how we can reconnect with our true nature.

Bhakti does not require us to abandon our ordinary responsibilities. You do not have to leave your job, give up your family life, or move to a cave. Most practitioners continue working, raising families, and participating in ordinary society. What changes is how we understand the purpose of our lives. Instead of allowing personal gratification, achievement, or social approval to dictate our priorities, we gradually learn to place a higher purpose at the center of our lives. That shift naturally influences how we use our time, which habits we cultivate, and what we consider important.

This course introduces the foundational ideas and practices of the bhakti tradition. We’ll explore what devotion means, how bhakti is practiced in daily life, and why it has long been understood as a complete path of yoga. We’ll look at the nature of the self and reality, the causes of suffering, and the practices through which spiritual realization develops. Later courses will explore these ideas in greater depth and build on this foundation.

Module 2 — How Bhakti Relates to Other Yogic Paths

The yoga tradition describes several paths, each emphasizing a different primary discipline:

Karma yoga centers on disciplined action. It teaches that work and responsibility can become a means of spiritual growth when performed without attachment to personal reward.

Jñāna yoga centers on philosophical inquiry. It emphasizes careful analysis of reality, discrimination between the temporary and the eternal, and insight into the nature of the self.

Dhyāna yoga centers on meditation. It trains attention, steadies the mind, and seeks sustained inward absorption. More recently developed systems such as rāja yoga, kriyā yoga, kuṇḍalinī, and aṣṭāṅga would fall under this broad category.

These paths are not considered rival or conflicting approaches. They use different methods to pursue spiritual realization. The path of bhakti is sometimes referred to as the culmination of these various paths because it incorporates their respective primary disciplines:

  • When bhakti is added to karma yoga, action is not only detached from personal reward; it can be offered as an act of devotion. For example, a teacher may begin by practicing karma yoga, doing their work responsibly without being attached to praise, promotion, or recognition. As they become more devotionally oriented, that same work can become an expression of bhakti. Before beginning the day, they may consciously offer their teaching to the Divine and see their work as an opportunity to serve. The lesson planning, teaching, and helping students remain the same, but the motivation has changed from simply performing one's duty without attachment to consciously serving the Divine through that work.
  • When bhakti is added to jñāna yoga, knowledge is not only analytical understanding; it culminates in recognition of a conscious source. For example, a philosopher may reason through study and reflection that consciousness cannot be reduced to matter. At first this realization remains primarily analytical. But as it deepens, the seeker recognizes that the ultimate source of consciousness must itself be conscious. At that point the orientation of the practice changes. The person may continue studying wisdom literature and engaging in philosophical inquiry, but now that study is done in a spirit of service and devotion to that conscious source.
  • When bhakti is added to dhyāna yoga, meditation practice takes a step beyond inward absorption; it becomes sustained awareness of our divine, conscious source. For example, someone might begin meditation by focusing on the breath to steady the mind. As the mind becomes more focused, they can direct that attention toward the Divine.

When we take up bhakti, ethical discipline, philosophical inquiry, and meditation are not set aside: they become natural expressions of the relationship bhakti seeks to uncover. Understanding this helps prevent two distortions. One is reducing bhakti to sentiment. The other is treating it as merely one technique among many. Within its own presentation, bhakti functions as a comprehensive orientation in which action, knowledge, and meditation operate together as aspects of a life centered on devotion.

Module 3 — A Quick Picture of “Who You Are”

Yoga philosophy begins with a distinction between the self and the body. The body is the vehicle through which the self currently functions, but it is not the self itself.

Our bodies change throughout our lives, and so do our thoughts, emotions, preferences, and sense of identity. Yet beneath all of these changes, we experience ourselves as the same person. That enduring observer is referred to as the soul (ātma): the conscious, individual self.

The soul is described as eternal and unchanging. It does not develop, deteriorate, or transform in response to material conditions. What changes are the bodily and mental coverings through which the self operates. The self itself remains constant, and its existence is not terminated by the death of the physical body.

According to the yoga wisdom literatures, the self continues beyond bodily death. This continued existence may involve further material embodiment, a process commonly called reincarnation, unless and until the self becomes free from material identification and attachment.

Bhakti also distinguishes between the self and what it calls false ego (ahaṅkāra). False ego does not necessarily mean arrogance. It refers to the constructed identity that arises from identifying the self with the material body and its temporary designations, such as profession, nationality, interests, temperament, or physical attributes.

The teachings surrounding false ego (ahaṅkāra) do not mean that at some point we cease to exist as individuals. Ātma refers to the self as it actually is: an eternal, individual conscious being. Spiritual growth does not eliminate individuality. It means becoming less identified with the temporary body and its designations and more aware of the self’s actual nature in relation to its source.

These ideas come from a substantial body of wisdom literature and centuries of philosophical commentary. At the same time, these conclusions are not meant to remain theoretical: they are testable through sustained practice. A later course will explore the philosophical arguments in greater detail. For now, the important point is to understand the basic framework on which bhakti rests:

  • You’re not the temporary body
  • You are an eternal individual soul
  • You have an eternal, loving relationship with your divine, conscious source
  • Full awareness of spiritual reality is obscured by bodily identification and material attachments

The aim is not to prove a position but to give language to a lived intuition many thoughtful people already feel:

“There’s more to me than my appetites. There’s more to life than what my nervous system demands.”

Bhakti speaks to that “more.”

Module 4 — Satisfying the Soul

The self is eternal, but the things we usually look to for happiness are temporary. We can pursue pleasure, comfort, romance, camaraderie, recognition, achievement, security, and other forms of material happiness, but none of them can provide lasting fulfillment. We may enjoy them for a time, but the enjoyment fades and we eventually want something more.

This helps explain why dissatisfaction is not always a failure of effort. Sometimes we are looking for lasting fulfillment in things that cannot provide it. If the self is eternal and conscious, then temporary material experiences cannot fully satisfy its deeper nature.

This does not mean that material things are inherently bad. Work, relationships, possessions, recreation, and other ordinary parts of life can provide legitimate forms of happiness and serve important purposes. The problem comes when we expect them to provide the lasting fulfillment that only spiritual realization can provide.

As this understanding develops, our priorities can begin to change. We may become less interested in pursuing things solely for pleasure, recognition, or personal advancement and more interested in how our time, abilities, relationships, and resources can support our spiritual development. The things we do may remain much the same, but our reasons for doing them can change.

This is where bhakti becomes a way of life. The practices of bhakti help us develop the vision of eternity: seeing ourselves and every other living entity as eternal spiritual beings, kindred spirits. Over time, devotion can reshape what we consider meaningful, how we define success, and what we choose to give our energy to.

Application

Choose one ordinary action today and consciously dedicate it to the Divine. Observe whether this shift in intention alters your experience of the action.

Module 5 — The Core Practices

Because bhakti centers relationship, it is supported by practices that nurture and stabilize that connection. In everyday life, bhakti takes shape through:

  • Mantra meditation (japa) — the personal, repeated chanting of a sacred mantra, often on beads. This practice trains attention and anchors the mind in remembrance. It is typically performed individually and consistently. The next module explains mantra meditation in more depth.
  • Kīrtan — congregational chanting with musical accompaniment, traditionally in a call-and-response format. Practicing together creates a shared spiritual experience and can deepen our attention, enthusiasm, and sense of connection.
  • Hearing — study of sacred texts (śāstra) and exposure to established teachers within the tradition. Hearing provides conceptual clarity and prevents practice from becoming purely emotional or mechanical.
  • Service (sevā) — deliberate acts offered for a purpose beyond self-interest. Service may be formal or informal, public or private, but it is intended to align action with devotion.
  • Worship and offerings — structured ways of bringing devotion into daily rhythms, such as offering food before eating or setting aside space for prayer.

These practices are meant to work together. Meditation steadies attention and makes study more focused. Study deepens our understanding and gives greater purpose to meditation. Service puts what we learn into practice, while practicing with others strengthens our focus and enthusiasm. Daily offerings bring devotion into ordinary activities rather than limiting it to dedicated practice sessions. Over time, the practices begin to support and strengthen one another.

At the same time, they do not need to be adopted all at once. Most people begin with one or two steady commitments and allow their practice to grow gradually. What matters at the beginning is consistency. As practice becomes established, other practices can be added naturally.

Reflection

Reflect back on your life. Where have you sought connection? How has that changed over time? Notice how you invest your free time. What helps you feel connected and seen? What tends to isolate you? Which of these practices do you think would resonate with you the most?

Module 6 — The Hare Krishna Maha Mantra

Among the practices described so far, mantra meditation holds a central place. In this lineage, the Hare Krishna maha mantra is emphasized:

Hare Krishna, Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna, Hare Hare
Hare Rama, Hare Rama, Rama Rama, Hare Hare

These are names for our divine, conscious source in the Sanskrit language. They don’t conflict with other names from other traditions. The yoga wisdom literatures explain that just as people in different parts of the world point at the same sun and call it by different names, cultures across time have used different names to address the same ultimate reality. Traditions differ in their details, but this Hare Krishna mantra is understood to be calling out to the Cause of all Causes.

Krishna means “all-attractive,” reminding us that we’re calling out not to “a god,” but to God: the personal source of everything we’re attracted to. Hare invokes the divine feminine, pure love personified. Rama means “the source of all pleasure,” pointing toward the deep joy of a restored spiritual relationship. The name also evokes the spiritual strength and support that sustain our relationship with the Divine.

Taken together, this mantra is like calling out, “My friend, my friend! Please help me remember my relationship with you. Please help me to be a little less selfish. Please help me to be a little more selfless. Please engage me in some service.”

What makes this practice distinctive is its directness. The mantra is understood to be a personal address through sacred sound. In chanting, one is not just thinking about the Divine, but calling out and experiencing direct contact. The Divine is understood to be conscious and responsive, making this a living relationship rather than an abstract idea. That experience may be more apparent at some times than others. With consistent practice, awareness of the Divine can become more steady, gradually extending beyond the time spent chanting and into the rest of our lives.

Another feature of this practice is its portability. The mantra can be memorized easily and carried with you wherever you go. It does not depend on a particular location or setup. You can chant it quietly while walking, driving, waiting in line, or sitting at home. No one has to authorize it. You can call out anytime you like.

Because this practice is based on direct engagement, it does not require prior philosophical mastery. One need not resolve every theological question before beginning. The tradition invites experiment: chant with attention and observe the effects over time. If you are not ready to chant this mantra specifically, you may chant any sincere name of God you relate to. The point is we take time out of our day to sit and call out for personal connection without any ulterior motive.

Module 7 — Tradition Without Sectarianism

Bhakti is a living tradition with a body of philosophy, practices, texts, and teachings that have been transmitted across generations. This transmission is known as paramparā, or disciplic succession. Sacred texts preserve the teachings, teachers help students understand and apply them, and communities provide an environment in which the practices can be lived out.

We rely on this kind of transmission throughout life. We learn a language from people who speak it, a craft from those who have practiced it, and medicine or law through established bodies of knowledge and trained teachers. We begin by learning from those who have developed and practiced it before us.

The same principle applies to bhakti. Learning within a lineage gives us a framework for understanding the philosophy and practices as they have been taught and practiced over time. It also provides continuity, helping prevent the tradition from gradually becoming whatever an individual happens to find most appealing or convenient.

This does not require rejecting other spiritual paths or claiming that bhakti is superior to them. It means approaching bhakti on its own terms before deciding what we accept, question, or continue to explore.

Reflection

Many of us have become skeptical of spiritual teachers and ancient texts, while also striving to avoid sectarianism. This can make it difficult to study a particular worldview in depth. At the same time, without accepting an authority greater than our own materially conditioned minds, it can be difficult to transcend our conditioning. Is it possible to study and experience a tradition as it is without abandoning our discernment?

Module 8 — What Bhakti Does Over Time

When bhakti becomes consistent, it does not remain confined to private belief or isolated moments of practice. It begins to influence both inner life and outward conduct. Because it reorients what is treated as central, it gradually affects how one relates to everything else.

Over time, practitioners often observe:

  • A clearer sense of purpose grounded in service rather than self-advancement
  • A more stable experience of meaning that does not collapse when circumstances change
  • A deeper form of contentment not entirely dependent on material conditions
  • Greater steadiness of attention and reduced internal fragmentation
  • Strengthening of traits such as humility, restraint, gratitude, and compassion
  • A reduced need to measure oneself against others
  • More consistency between stated values and lived behavior
  • A shift in how success, failure, praise, and criticism are interpreted

These developments are not cosmetic: they affect how a person speaks, works, handles conflict, uses time, and relates to others. Bhakti does not remove personality or erase individuality. It reorganizes them within a different paradigm, and that shift gradually becomes visible in daily life.

Module 9 — The 21 Day Spiritual Fitness Challenge

Understanding ideas rarely changes much on its own. What makes a difference is repeated engagement, so this course offers an optional, time-bound experiment called the 21 Day Spiritual Fitness Challenge.

To opt-in, choose one of the following levels and maintain it steadily for 21 days.

Level 1 — 10 minutes per day

Spend five minutes chanting and five minutes reading or listening to a short bhakti teaching.

Level 2 — 20 minutes per day

Spend ten minutes chanting and ten minutes hearing or reading.

Level 3 — 30 or more minutes per day

Spend fifteen to twenty minutes chanting, ten minutes hearing or reading, and five minutes in brief reflection or journaling.

The level matters less than consistency. A modest daily commitment is more useful than a longer practice that is difficult to sustain.

How to Chant

Here is the Hare Krishna maha mantra again for convenience:

Hare Krishna, Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna, Hare Hare
Hare Rama, Hare Rama, Rama Rama, Hare Hare

The mantra can be spoken quietly to yourself or sung aloud. Some prefer a steady, meditative repetition, while others find it helpful to sing with melody.

Many practitioners use a strand of beads (a mālā) to keep track of repetitions, but this is not required. You can set a timer and chant for the allotted time.

A later course in this curriculum explores mantra meditation in greater depth. For this 21-day experiment, focus on establishing a steady practice and observing what develops over time.

What to Hear or Read

Keep the hearing component manageable. Choose one source you can engage with consistently rather than constantly switching between different materials. This could be additional coursework from this curriculum, a recorded talk (check out Srila Prabhupada or Vaisesika Dasa), or a single text that you work through gradually. We recommend Bhagavad Gita: Talks Between the Soul and God. The goal is to go deeper through repeated engagement rather than trying to get through as much material as possible.

Weekly Addition

If possible, include one point of community contact each week. This could involve attending a kīrtan, visiting a temple, meeting with a practitioner, or joining an online discussion. Shared practice often stabilizes individual effort.

How to Evaluate the 21 Days

The goal is to spend 21 days giving bhakti a consistent place in your life and observing what develops. Did you feel some reciprocation in the heart?

Module 10 — Reconnection

Quote

Srila Prabhupada writes in The Nectar of Devotion (1970):

“The basic principle of the living condition is that we have a general propensity to love someone. No one can live without loving someone else. This propensity is present in every living being. Even an animal like a tiger has this loving propensity at least in a dormant stage, and it is certainly present in the human beings. The missing point, however, is where to repose our love so that everyone can become happy. At the present moment the human society teaches one to love his country or family or his personal self, but there is no information where to repose the loving propensity so that everyone can become happy.”

This course has presented bhakti as a response to that “missing point.” If the self is enduring and conscious, and if the Divine is also conscious, then the impulse to love is not accidental. It has an object.

Key Takeaways
  • You are more than your body and mind.
  • The longing to love and be loved points somewhere real.
  • What you place at the center of your life shapes who you become.
  • Devotion is a shift in priority, not an escape from responsibility.
  • Daily spiritual practice makes that shift tangible.
  • Tradition protects the path from becoming diluted over time.
  • Change often comes through steady practice as opposed to dramatic experiences.
  • Our relationship with the Divine is not created by practice; it is revealed through it.
  • The goal of bhakti is not self-improvement, but awakened love.

Bhakti ultimately proposes that the longing for connection and fulfillment is not solved by acquisition or achievement, but by reorienting love toward its proper object. That proposal cannot be resolved theoretically. It is tested through practice over time.

Bhakti explains that the Divine does not force relationship. Love cannot be compelled. If we prefer to remain absorbed in temporary pursuits, we are allowed that freedom. The world provides ample opportunity for exploration and ambition.

At the same time, the tradition maintains that Reality—distinguished from illusion—is always available. If and when a person becomes dissatisfied with temporary centers of meaning and wants something enduring, the relationship has not disappeared. It can be consciously reengaged.

From this perspective, devotion is a voluntary decision about what we will center our lives around.
